Why Deterministic Legal AI Beats Probabilistic Legal AI Under the EU AI Act

A closer look at traceability, verification, human oversight, and why architecture may matter more than compliance paperwork.

The bill for probabilistic legal AI is already overdue

Most "legal AI" sold today is a thin wrapper over a general-purpose language model. Ask it a question and it generates a plausible-sounding answer, token by token.

It is very good at sounding like a lawyer and delivering advice with confidence, even when that advice is wrong. But it is not, by design, built to be independently checked in the same way as a lawyer's written advice.

That gap is precisely what makes traceability, transparency, and human oversight such important parts of the EU AI Act's high-risk framework.

There is another approach that remains relatively uncommon in legal AI: deterministic systems, where outputs are produced by fixed, inspectable rules operating against a citation-backed corpus rather than generated from a language model.

The same inputs produce the same outputs, and the steps between them remain visible and inspectable.

ALIS is built around this approach. The architecture underneath the system determines how much compliance, verification, and accountability can be achieved in the first place.

Start with what is happening in courtrooms right now, because it is the clearest evidence of the problem underneath the technology. A public tracker maintained by HEC Paris researcher Damien Charlotin has recorded well over a thousand court decisions worldwide in which parties relied on AI-generated case law, quotations, or statutes that did not exist or were materially wrong.

The consequences have escalated accordingly. What began with the $5,000 sanction in Mata v. Avianca in 2023 has expanded into six-figure aggregate penalties, bar suspensions, and disciplinary referrals.

Purpose-built, paid legal research products have also generated fabricated quotations that made their way into briefs. Wrapping a general-purpose generative model in legal branding does not change what it fundamentally is: a system that produces the statistically likely next token, not a system that knows the difference between a real citation and a convincing one.

What actually changed under the Act, and when

The EU AI Act's Annex III classifies certain AI systems used in the administration of justice as high-risk. The scope is narrower than the broader "legal AI" market: it specifically covers systems used by judicial authorities, or on their behalf, to research and apply facts and law to cases, with a comparable provision for alternative dispute resolution.

A law firm using AI on its own client matters does not automatically fall within that specific high-risk category as currently written.

But the distinction matters less than it first appears. The functions the Act identifies — researching facts and law, interpreting them, and applying law to specific circumstances — are also central to firm-side legal AI.

The regulatory boundary may distinguish courts from law firms, but the underlying risk does not disappear at that boundary.

THE BROADER SHIFT Legal AI is moving toward a model where traceability, verification, and accountability are becoming requirements of the system itself, not merely compliance paperwork layered on afterward.

The Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) entered into force on 27 July 2026, moving the main compliance date for standalone Annex III high-risk systems from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027.

That delay should not be mistaken for a reduction in risk. It reflects a more practical problem: the harmonised technical standards needed to assess conformity are not yet ready. Regulators still need the infrastructure and methods to audit these systems against the Act's requirements.

What the requirements actually ask for

Strip the Act's high-risk requirements down to their essentials, and four demands stand out:

FOUR DEMANDS traceability · transparency · meaningful human oversight · reliable accuracy and robustness

Article 14 on human oversight is particularly relevant to legal AI. It requires deployers to understand a system's capabilities and limitations, correctly interpret its outputs, and guard against automation bias — the tendency to place undue trust in confident-looking machine-generated results.

The hallucination cases described above are automation bias in practice: lawyers trusted outputs they had no reliable way to independently verify.

You cannot meaningfully oversee what you cannot inspect.

That makes architecture more than a technical detail. A generative model's fluency is not an audit trail. Asking an LLM to explain why it produced an answer generates another plausible explanation; it does not necessarily reveal the actual process that produced the original output.

There is no fixed rule set an auditor can inspect and say, "this is why the system reached this conclusion."

A deterministic system approaches the requirement differently. Its reasoning process is the system's actual execution: defined rules operating on identifiable, citation-backed data.

What the system did and what it can show you it did are therefore the same thing.

Traceability is not something added around the system; it is a property of the system itself.

What that looks like in practice

This is the architecture ALIS is built around, not merely a positioning statement: no LLM in the reasoning pipeline.

Every fact is traceable to a specific source document and page, rather than inferred from a model's learned approximation of the law. Every output includes a visible reasoning trace showing the actual steps used to reach it, not a generated explanation produced afterward.

A deterministic three-tier guardrail — safe, caution, or review — evaluates every query and response before it reaches the user.

When a matter requires solicitor judgment rather than automated analysis, ALIS stops and routes it for review instead of producing a confident-looking answer.

THE DIFFERENCE A disclaimer says: "we might be wrong."

A guardrail says: "we know when the evidence is insufficient to answer."

A disclaimer telling users that an AI output "may be inaccurate" does not determine when the system should be trusted. It simply shifts the burden of detection to the user.

A guardrail that evaluates outputs against predefined criteria and withholds an answer when the matter exceeds the system's defined limits makes a fundamentally different claim.

What determinism doesn't buy you

None of this amounts to a compliance certificate, and any vendor suggesting otherwise should be treated skeptically.

Conformity assessment under the Act is a separate regulatory process, and the December 2027 deadline for Annex III systems has not arrived, regardless of architecture.

Determinism also has genuine trade-offs. A rule-based system can only handle what it has been engineered to understand, and expanding that scope requires deliberate engineering rather than simply increasing a model's training data.

It cannot improvise fluently outside its domain. It will tell you when something falls outside its scope — which is precisely the point, but still a meaningful limitation compared with generative AI.

What determinism provides is therefore narrower, but more useful, than a compliance certificate: a stronger architectural foundation for the properties the regulatory direction increasingly demands — traceability, reproducibility, and meaningful human oversight.

Those properties do not have to be added after the fact by a compliance team. They are consequences of how the system is built.

The question worth asking now

Moving the Annex III deadline to December 2027 is not a reason to stop paying attention.

If anything, it highlights how difficult it is to verify the compliance of opaque AI systems at scale. The regulatory framework is still catching up with the challenge of auditing systems whose outputs are difficult to reproduce or trace.

Firms evaluating legal AI do not need to wait for that process to finish. The more useful question to ask a vendor today is simpler:

Can you show me exactly why your system produced this answer — not a generated explanation of why, but the actual rules, evidence, and reasoning steps that produced it?

If the answer is no, the problem goes beyond future compliance.

It is a limitation inherent in the system's architecture, and one that should concern anyone relying on it for consequential legal decisions.
